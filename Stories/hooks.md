# Hooks

The first line decides whether the rest is watched. It has one job: open a gap
between what the viewer knows and what they want to know, and open it fast.

## What a hook has to do

- Land in the first 1–2 seconds; no channel intro, no "in this video".
- Raise one question the viewer now needs answered.
- Promise the answer is coming, and not give it away.
- Be true. A hook the video does not pay off costs the next video's views.

## Shapes that work

| Shape | Example opening |
|---|---|
| The impossible fact | "This village appears on no map after 1961." |
| The countdown | "Forty seconds after take-off, both engines stopped." |
| The cost | "It took eleven years and two fortunes to build. It stood for one night." |
| The contradiction | "Everyone there heard the sound. Nobody could say where it came from." |
| The direct question | "What happens to a town when the one factory closes?" |
| The small detail | "The letter was signed with the wrong name. That was the first clue." |
| In the middle | Open on the loudest moment, then go back: one bridge line, "Two years earlier…" |

## Shapes that lose people

- "Hi guys, welcome back to the channel."
- "Before we start, like and subscribe."
- Explaining what the video will cover instead of starting it.
- A question too broad to care about: "Have you ever wondered about history?"
- A hook whose answer is in the title and nowhere else.

## After the hook

- Line 2 raises the stakes or narrows the question; it does not restate line 1.
- The answer, if there is one, arrives at the end — not at second ten.
- Each section ends on something unresolved, so there is a reason to stay.
- The last line lands the point, and does not trail off into a plug.

## For the script file

Write the hook as the first take's first line, with `hero` pointing at the
single image or card it belongs to. One hero per line — the camera can only be
on one thing, and the spotlight dims the rest.
